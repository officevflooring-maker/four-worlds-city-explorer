Ancient Rome

Ancient Rome was a real city. These pictures are artistic illustrations, so each place separates what history tells us from what the artist imagined.

Look around the city

Select a building to learn about it. Every place is also listed below.

Historical notes are kept short and link to their sources. The buildings shown are illustrations, not exact reconstructions.

All 16 places

  • Aqueduct

    Carries fresh water from distant hills into the city.

    Look closer at the Aqueduct

    Look closer

    Look at the channel on top of the arches. Which way would the water flow?

    History What history tells us

    Roman aqueducts moved water by gravity along a gentle downhill slope. Most of their length ran underground; arches carried the channel across valleys.

    Source: Encyclopaedia Britannica: Aqueduct

    Art In this artwork

    This short arched section is a simplified illustration, not a specific aqueduct.

    Question to consider

    Why might engineers keep most of a water channel underground?

  • Public fountain

    A shared place where water from the aqueducts could be collected.

    Look closer at the Public fountain

    Look closer

    Find where the water enters and where it overflows.

    History What history tells us

    Frontinus, who was in charge of Rome's water supply around AD 97, wrote a detailed account of the city's aqueducts and how their water was managed.

    Source: LacusCurtius, University of Chicago: Frontinus: The Aqueducts of Rome (text and translation)

    Art In this artwork

    The fountain's design is the artist's own.

    Question to consider

    Who would use a public fountain, and how often?

  • Public baths

    A place to wash, exercise and meet people.

    Look closer at the Public baths

    Look closer

    Notice the steam and the large pool. What would it take to heat that much water?

    History What history tells us

    Roman public baths (thermae) usually had a cold room (frigidarium), a warm room (tepidarium) and a hot room (caldarium). Floors were often warmed by hot air flowing underneath, a system called a hypocaust.

    Source: Encyclopaedia Britannica: Thermae

    Art In this artwork

    The layout and decoration here are imagined.

    Question to consider

    Roman baths were for washing and for meeting people. What places do both jobs today?

  • Amphitheater

    A huge oval arena for public shows.

    Look closer at the Amphitheater

    Look closer

    Count the levels of arches. Why build so many?

    History What history tells us

    The Flavian Amphitheater in Rome, now called the Colosseum, was dedicated around AD 80. It hosted animal hunts and gladiator fights, and seating was arranged by social rank.

    Source: Smarthistory: The Colosseum

    Art In this artwork

    This arena resembles the Colosseum but is an artistic illustration.

    Question to consider

    Seats were arranged by social rank. How do stadiums decide who sits where today?

  • Racetrack (circus)

    A long track where crowds watched chariot races.

    Look closer at the Racetrack (circus)

    Look closer

    Look at the barrier down the middle of the track. What is it for?

    History What history tells us

    Chariot races were the main events at Rome's Circus Maximus, a long racetrack between the Palatine and Aventine hills.

    Source: Encyclopaedia Britannica: Circus (racecourse)

    Art In this artwork

    The track shown is much smaller than the real Circus Maximus.

    Question to consider

    Why might a long, narrow shape suit chariot racing?

  • Apartment block (insula)

    Homes stacked several floors high, often with shops below.

    Look closer at the Apartment block (insula)

    Look closer

    Look for balconies, stairs and shop awnings.

    History What history tells us

    Many city dwellers lived in multi-story apartment blocks called insulae. Rooms facing the street were often used as shops, and fire was a constant danger.

    Source: Smarthistory: Roman domestic architecture: the insula

    Art In this artwork

    Colors, plants and balconies are the artist's interpretation.

    Question to consider

    What would make living on an upper floor easier or harder?

  • Courtyard house (domus)

    A family house built around open-air spaces.

    Look closer at the Courtyard house (domus)

    Look closer

    Find the open courtyard in the middle. What does it let in?

    History What history tells us

    Wealthier families might live in a domus, a house arranged around open spaces such as an atrium that brought in light and air.

    Source: Smarthistory: Roman domestic architecture: the domus

    Art In this artwork

    This is a general illustration, not a specific house.

    Question to consider

    This house faces inward, toward its courtyard. What are the benefits of that?

  • Domed temple

    A temple with a great round dome.

    Look closer at the Domed temple

    Look closer

    Look at the very top of the dome.

    History What history tells us

    The Pantheon in Rome has a concrete dome with an open circle at the top, called the oculus. It is the building's main source of direct light.

    Source: Smarthistory: The Pantheon, Rome

    Art In this artwork

    This temple is inspired by the Pantheon but is not an exact copy.

    Question to consider

    How else could a large building without many windows let in light?

    A Roman basilica was a large covered hall used as a law court and for banking and business.

    Look closer

    History What history tells us

    Romans built arches to commemorate victories and important events. The Arch of Titus in Rome, built shortly after AD 81, is one surviving example.

    The Tower of Hercules in A Coruña, Spain, was built as a Roman lighthouse in the late 1st century AD and is still in use today.
