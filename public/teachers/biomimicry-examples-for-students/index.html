20 biomimicry examples for students

Grades
4-12
Time
1 class period
Subjects
Science, engineering, design

Free to print and share for teaching, in a classroom or a homeschool. Please credit The Atlas of Cities.

Quick answer

Biomimicry means solving human problems by learning from nature. Famous examples include Velcro (copied from burrs), self-cleaning paint (from lotus leaves), Japan's quieter bullet train (from a kingfisher's beak), the first airplane controls (from birds' wings) and buildings cooled like termite mounds. The table below has 20 examples.

20 examples of biomimicry

Each row names a plant or animal, the trick it uses to survive and something people made after studying it. Some of these are in shops today; others are still being tested in labs.

NatureWhat it doesWhat people made
Burdock burrsTiny hooks catch on animal fur, so the seeds travel.Velcro: engineer George de Mestral copied the hooks after burrs stuck to his dog in the 1940s.
Lotus leafTiny waxy bumps make water roll off and carry dirt away.Self-cleaning paint and glass.
KingfisherIts long, pointed beak dives into water with hardly a splash.The nose of Japan's 500 Series bullet train (1997), quieter when it leaves a tunnel.
OwlComb-like edges on its wing feathers make its flight almost silent.A quieter pantograph (the arm that touches the power line) on the same train.
Termite moundTunnels and chimneys let warm air out and keep the nest cool.The Eastgate Centre in Harare, Zimbabwe (1996), cooled with no ordinary air conditioning.
Humpback whaleBumps on the front edge of its flippers help it turn tightly.Bumpy-edged blades for fans and wind turbines.
Shark skinIt is covered in tiny tooth-like scales.Surface patterns that make it hard for germs to settle, and racing swimsuits.
GeckoMillions of tiny hairs on its toes let it climb walls and ceilings.Dry sticky tapes and robot grippers that leave no glue behind.
MusselIts threads stick to wet rocks in crashing waves.Glues that work under water and in the body.
Namib desert beetleBumps on its back collect drops of water from fog.Surfaces designed to gather water from fog.
BirdsThey twist their wingtips to turn and keep their balance.The Wright brothers' wing warping, which steered the first airplane in 1903.
Cat's eyesThey shine back in the dark when light hits them.Road reflectors called cat's eyes (1934): inventor Percy Shaw said a cat's eyes gave him the idea.
Honeybee combSix-sided wax cells hold the most honey with the least wax.Light, strong honeycomb panels in airplanes and doors.
Moth eyeTiny bumps stop light reflecting, so no shine gives the moth away.Anti-glare coatings for screens and solar panels.
Morpho butterflyIts bright blue comes from tiny structures in its wings, not from color.Colors made without dye, for fabrics and screens.
MosquitoIts jagged mouthparts slide into skin with little pain.Thinner, less painful needles.
Spider webSome silk reflects ultraviolet light that birds can see.Window glass with ultraviolet patterns, so birds don't fly into it.
BoxfishIts boxy body slips smoothly through water.The Mercedes-Benz Bionic concept car (2005).
Maple seedIt spins like a helicopter blade as it falls.Small single-wing drones that spin as they fly.
SunflowerIts seeds are packed in spirals with no space wasted.Mirror layouts for solar power plants that use less land (a 2012 MIT study).

To turn it into a game, print the page and fold the last column back. Students read the first two columns and guess the invention.

Biomimicry in buildings and cities

Architects use biomimicry too. The Eastgate Centre copies a termite mound's airflow, and green roofs and rain gardens soak up rain the way a forest floor does. In Meghalaya, India, people go one step further: they guide the roots of living fig trees across rivers into bridges that grow stronger with age.

The Green World on this site imagines a whole town built this way: a tower that breathes like a termite mound, a hall grown from a living tree, cottages with plant roofs, and each building paired with a real nature-based technology. The building pages below show it.

Classroom design challenge

  • Pick a problem: staying cool, keeping clean, saving water, moving quietly.
  • Ask "How does nature do this?" and research one plant or animal that solves it.
  • Sketch an invention or building feature based on it.
  • Build a simple model and test it.
  • Present: the problem, the organism, the design and the test results.

Questions students and teachers ask

What is biomimicry in simple words?

Copying good ideas from plants and animals to solve human problems.

What is the most famous example of biomimicry?

Velcro is probably the best known. It was inspired by burrs that stuck to a dog's fur.

What are 20 examples of biomimicry?

Velcro (burrs), self-cleaning paint (lotus leaves), the bullet train nose (kingfisher), quiet train parts (owls), the Eastgate Centre (termite mounds), turbine blades (humpback whales), germ-resistant surfaces (shark skin), dry tape (geckos), underwater glue (mussels), fog catchers (desert beetles), airplane controls (birds), road reflectors (cat's eyes), honeycomb panels (bees), anti-glare coatings (moth eyes), dye-free color (morpho butterflies), gentler needles (mosquitoes), bird-safe glass (spider webs), a concept car (boxfish), spinning drones (maple seeds) and solar plant layouts (sunflowers).

What is an example of biomimicry in a building?

The Eastgate Centre in Harare, Zimbabwe, copies how termite mounds move air, so it stays cool with no ordinary air conditioning.

Is biomimicry the same as using natural materials?

No. Biomimicry copies how nature solves a problem, like the shape of a beak or the bumps on a leaf. A building can be made of steel and glass and still use biomimicry.
